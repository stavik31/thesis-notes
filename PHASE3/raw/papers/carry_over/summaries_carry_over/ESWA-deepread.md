## Sarraf & McGuire 2020 — Integration and Comparison of MCDM Methods in a Safe Route Planner ★ ROUTER + EVAL TEMPLATE

### Context grounding
The paper's opening is our pitch verbatim: existing risk maps "can be confusing and must be
manually interpreted by drivers," and "Google Maps and Waze present two or three alternative
paths … based on the travel time and distance" with no safety term. Their fix is an **Expert
Navigation System** that adds *safety* as a routing criterion alongside time and distance and
returns a *ranked* set of routes. This confirms route planning as a **real consumer slot** for
a per-segment risk score: the risk engine (Jiang/Gao) sits *upstream*, Sarraf's MCDM layer sits
*downstream*, and the thesis threads vehicle type through both.

Three context bricks the deep read nails down:

1. **It assumes the risk score already exists.** Sarraf does not model crash risk from scratch;
   it consumes a per-segment **Weighted Crash Rate (WCR)** and ranks routes. So the division of
   labour is explicit and clean: *someone else builds the risk surface, Sarraf routes on it.*
   The thesis builds a **vehicle-type-specific** risk surface and feeds it into exactly this slot.
2. **It routes everyone the same.** The WCR (below) normalises crash counts by **AADT** (traffic
   exposure) but has **no vehicle-type dimension** — a motorcycle and an HGV get the identical
   route for the same O–D and the same user weights. This is the aggregate-routing baseline the
   headline divergence test diverges *from*.
3. **It supplies the evaluation tradition.** Spearman / Average Overlap / DCG for comparing
   ranked route lists — the metrics our routing-quality evaluation adopts wholesale.

### Technical grounding (the machinery we reuse)
**(a) Path generation.** Dijkstra produces M alternative paths between source and destination:
A = shortest, B = fastest, C = safest, then D…M = variants made by **removing the highest-crash
edge** on the shortest/fastest path and re-running Dijkstra. So "alternatives" are generated by
*ablating risky edges* — a cheap, reusable trick for producing a candidate set to rank.

**(b) The risk score — Weighted Crash Rate (WCR).** Per segment, severity-weighted crash count
normalised by exposure:

```
WCR(ni,nj) = [ Fatal·30 + Injury·15 + PDO·5 + RealTime·2 ] · 100000 / (AADT · 365) + 1
```

Always ≥ 1 (1 = no crashes). **This is the same design move as Gao's severity weighting
(minor/serious/fatal = 1/2/3) — collapse multi-class severity into one scalar per segment** —
just with heavier weights and an explicit **AADT exposure denominator**. The thesis's move is to
compute this WCR-style scalar **per vehicle type** (motorcycle-WCR, HGV-WCR, …), ideally with a
*per-type* exposure denominator (per-type AADT/DfT counts) rather than one shared AADT — that
exposure-normalisation-by-type is itself a defensible technical contribution.

**(c) Safety weight for routing.** `SW = AW · WCR` where AW = travel time in seconds; this is the
edge weight Dijkstra minimises for the "safest" path (safe *and* fast). For the per-path criteria,
the route's total safety level = Σ WCR·haversine-distance over edges; total time and total distance
computed separately. So each candidate route becomes a 3-tuple (safety, time, distance) — the MCDM
decision matrix.

**(d) MCDM ranking — five methods compared.** AHP, Fuzzy AHP, TOPSIS, Fuzzy TOPSIS, PROMETHEE rank
the candidate routes given user pairwise preferences over {safety, time, distance}. Findings worth
keeping:
- **AHP and PROMETHEE give identical rankings and are the most reliable**; AHP recommended as the
  simple, robust default (PROMETHEE needs AHP to derive its weights anyway, and its net-flow output
  in [−1,1] is confusing).
- **TOPSIS and Fuzzy TOPSIS are unreliable in this setting** (their normalisation can rank the
  high-crash fastest path too highly).
- Best practice for Fuzzy TOPSIS: derive triangular weights with Fuzzy AHP and use them directly;
  *don't* fuzzify crisp AHP weights.
- The system can override the user: even when the user weights time ~2× over safety, the methods
  refuse to recommend a fastest path that is ~5× riskier and instead pick a safe-and-fast compromise.

**(e) Routing-quality evaluation — the template we adopt.**
- **Spearman's rank correlation** — simple, but weights top and bottom of the list equally → flagged
  as insufficient for routing (the top route matters most).
- **Average Overlap (AO)** — mean set-overlap at increasing depths; weights the top of the list
  more. For 5 unique routes the worst case (full reversal) ≈ 0.416, not 0.
- **Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG / nDCG)** — relevance scores (e.g. {5,4,1,−4,−5}, with *negative*
  scores penalising a bad route ranked highly) discounted by log_b(rank). **DCG is their recommended
  method** because it (i) weights the top of the list and (ii) explicitly *punishes a dangerous route
  placed near the top* — exactly the property a safe-route evaluator needs.

**Borrow (directly):** WCR-style per-segment severity+exposure scalar (computed **per vehicle
type**); Dijkstra-with-edge-ablation candidate generation; AHP (and/or PROMETHEE) as the MCDM
ranker; **Spearman + Average Overlap + DCG** as the routing-quality metric suite, with **DCG as the
headline** rank metric. **Extend:** add the vehicle-type axis to the risk term and (ideally) to the
exposure denominator; the "does the ranked route list differ by vehicle type for the same O–D" test
is the counterfactual the whole thesis turns on. Tag: `router` + `eval-template`.

## Gap test (Phase 3)
**Sarraf 2020:** full risk→time→distance MCDM router with a real rank-evaluation methodology — and
**no vehicle-type term** anywhere (AADT-normalised but vehicle-blind). NO. This is the strongest
single "the slot is open" brick: the routing machinery is mature and validated, the vehicle-type
dimension is simply absent. The thesis = **Sarraf's router + a per-vehicle-type risk surface (a
Gao/Jiang engine made type-aware), evaluated with Sarraf's own DCG-led metric suite.**
