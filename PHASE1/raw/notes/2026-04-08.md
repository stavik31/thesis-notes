## Analysis, Suggestions & Plan of Action

### Verdict on your plan: build CrashSage first

**Not a bad idea — it's actually a good one, with one condition.**

The concern with "implement first, find the gap later" is usually that you waste weeks building something disconnected from a real contribution. But in your case, the implementation *is* the gap-finding mechanism. STATS19 has a different schema, a different jurisdiction, a 3-class severity structure (Fatal / Serious / Slight) instead of CrashSage's binary, and is an order of magnitude larger than the Washington State dataset. Every place the CrashSage pipeline breaks, misbehaves, or underperforms on your data is a gap. You'll discover them empirically rather than inferring them from papers — which is a stronger foundation for a thesis anyway.

The condition: **go in with specific diagnostic questions**, not just "see what happens." If you implement blindly, you'll get a number (Macro F1) and not know what it means. If you instrument for the right things from the start, the implementation teaches you exactly what to solve.

### What you need to know about STATS19 before writing a line of code

STATS19 is structured across three relational tables — almost identical to CrashSage's Washington State setup:

| STATS19 Table | CrashSage Equivalent | Contains |
|---------------|----------------------|---------|
| `accidents` | Crash table | Location, date/time, road type, weather, light, speed limit, junction |
| `vehicles` | Vehicle/Unit table | Vehicle type, manoeuvre, age of driver, sex of driver, engine capacity |
| `casualties` | Person table | Casualty severity, age, pedestrian/cyclist/car occupant |

The tabular-to-text transformation CrashSage built for WSDOT will need to be **fully rebuilt** for STATS19's column names, coding schemes, and UK-specific terminology. That rebuild is non-trivial and is itself a real piece of work — not a side task.

One important structural difference: STATS19 severity is **3-class** (Fatal, Serious, Slight), not binary. CrashSage collapsed to binary because of extreme class imbalance. You'll face the same decision with UK data — and the choice of whether to mirror CrashSage's binary collapse or tackle the harder 3-class problem is a thesis decision, not a preprocessing detail.

With 5 years of STATS19 data, you're looking at roughly 500,000–700,000 crash records. This is far larger than anything used in either paper. That's an advantage for training but introduces compute and sampling decisions that neither paper had to address.

### Your intuition about causation vs. outcome — keep it

Your note that "understanding the factors that caused the crash and in what degree is more important than knowing the result" is the most important thing you wrote. Both papers treat this as an afterthought (post-hoc attribution on a severity classifier). That tension — predicting outcomes vs. explaining causes — is real and largely unresolved in the field. Don't lose that thread. It may end up being the framing of your thesis contribution.

### The compute question you haven't raised yet

CrashSage fine-tuned LLaMA3-8B on 4× NVIDIA A6000 GPUs (48GB each) for 30 epochs. You need to answer early: **what GPU resources do you have access to?** Options in rough order of feasibility:

- University HPC cluster (most likely path — ask your supervisor)
- Google Colab Pro+ (A100 40GB, limited session time — doable with LoRA)
- Kaggle (2× T4 free — slow but possible for prototyping)

If you don't have access to significant compute, the approach shifts: you rely more on zero/few-shot prompting (which requires no fine-tuning) and establish that as your baseline, then fine-tune on a sampled subset. Know this before committing to a full CrashSage replication.

By this point you'll have seen where CrashSage's approach holds on UK data and where it doesn't. Common failure modes to look for:
- Does 3-class performance collapse compared to binary? (If so: class imbalance handling is the gap)
- Do gradient attributions surface nonsensical or UK-irrelevant factors? (If so: domain adaptation quality is the gap)
- Does the model struggle on certain crash types or road environments that don't appear in US data? (If so: cross-jurisdiction generalization is the gap)
- Is the performance gap between CatBoost and SFT LLaMA larger or smaller than in CrashSage? (If smaller: narrative quality / template design is the gap)

Whichever failure mode is most consistent and interesting — that's your thesis.

### One framing to keep in mind

CrashSage only beats CatBoost by 0.007 Macro F1. The entire argument for using LLMs in this domain rests on **interpretability quality, not accuracy**. If you replicate CrashSage on STATS19 and gradient attribution surfaces clear, human-readable, domain-relevant factors (speed zones, junction types, UK road geometry) in a way that SHAP on CatBoost doesn't — that *is* the contribution, even if the accuracy improvement is marginal. Frame the thesis around what the model reveals, not just what it predicts.
